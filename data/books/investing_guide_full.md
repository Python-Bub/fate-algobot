# Investing Guide (Full)

## Introduction

Investing is one of the most important tools for growing long-term wealth in order to achieve financial goals. This guide will give beginners a breakdown of all types of investing and how they all work. Investing is not a way to generate wealth overnight; rather a way to grow money long-term. While investing may seem similar to an incredibly simple way to earn money, not all investments will generate profit, and the point of this guide is to help beginners evaluate and weigh different investments.

## Investment Strategies

### Value Investing

#### Deep Value & Intrinsic Value

Deep value investing is the strategy of buying unpopular stocks that are ignored by Wall Street trading at a steep discount to their true price while the rest of the market sells it. Intrinsic value is the true value of a company calculated based on its assets, cashflow, and earnings, and the margin of safety is the buffer between the intrinsic value and the price you buy it for. The margin of safety works as a fallback if the calculated intrinsic value’s estimate was slightly off. In order to decide which stock to trade, the price-to-earnings ratio must maintain a historically low value, and the price-to-book ratio, comparing a company’s market price to its net assets, must be below one, meaning that the buying price is less than the accounting value of the company’s assets. The hardest challenge in deep value investing is distinguishing a genuine bargain from a stock that is actually performing badly and stays cheap or goes bankrupt because the underlying business of the stock is problematic, a value trap. In order to avoid value traps, investors look for companies with low debts and liquid assets, cash or financial assets that can be quickly converted into profit after the market rebounds. Intrinsic value is the true worth of an asset based on its future cash generation, completely independent of its current stock price. While market value is a temporary number, intrinsic value is the real anchor price that is not volatile. An asset is worth all the future profit it will generate in the future discounted for inflation because a dollar earned in the future will most likely be less than a dollar earned today. The formula for calculating is as follows:

$$IV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + r)^t} + \frac{TV}{(1 + r)^n}$$

Where
IV = Intrinsic Value
CFt = Cash flow in year t
r = Discount rate (Required annual return)
TV = Terminal Value (Estimated worth of the company beyond the forecast period)
n = Number of years in forecast period

The formula for calculating terminal value is as follows:

$$TV = \frac{CF_{n} \times (1 + g)}{r - g}$$

Where
g = Perpetual growth rate (Constant, estimated rate at which the company’s free cash flows to grow)

Warning: g must be less than r to keep away from an impossible negative value.

Using these formulas, intrinsic value can be properly calculated and utilized.

#### Graham Investing & Net-Net Investing

Graham investing is based on two foundational concepts. The first one is that tickets are intrinsically random and only when the price of a stock is ridiculously cheap should the stock be bought. The second pillar is that utilizing margins of safety are key to avoiding losses. The margins of safety between the price of a company per share in net cash and the price cheaper it was bought for are essential because even if the business struggles, the physical cash protects the investment from turning negative immediately. Net-Net stocks are companies trading lower than their Net Current Asset Value or the company’s estimated liquidation value. To find a Graham Net-Net, the Net Current Asset Value, which can be found by subtracting the total liabilities from the current assets, must be at least one and a half times larger than the value of the market capitalization or the price of the total company.

#### Buffett Investing & Contrarian Investing

Buffett Investing is about buying the best stocks that are the most known with nearly unstoppable competitive advantages against their rivals holding them forever. It is an economic moat that protects a company from rivals, allowing the company to maintain high profit margins for decades. Buffett investing also heavily relies on only investing in industries that one is very familiar with and thoroughly understands how they make money and function in order to avoid catastrophic losses and be the most experienced one in that industry to have an advantage over others. Instead of selling, when stocks grow on their own, it eliminates market randomness and lets compound interest take over. Moat companies can be identified through high switching costs, network effects, intangible assets, and cost advantages. Contrarian investing relies on buying into a company when it starts dipping and hedge funds are forced to withdraw because their bank has to protect itself, but contrarian investing necessitates that the headline be only a temporary hurdle instead of a fatal blow. A temporary issue or a single bad earnings quarter where the price drops, but the company’s long-term investing remains intact is when contrarian investors would invest. The risk of being the only one buying a bad stock still exists, so contrarian investors prove mathematically, independently from the rest of the people selling, that the crowd is mathematically wrong.

#### Asset-Based Investing & Sum-of-the-Parts Valuation

Asset-based investing is an investment strategy that completely focuses on the company’s present form and fully ignores the future in order to find a stock that would be at profit if it were fully purchased and liquidated. The stock is held until a catalyst or a specific event that will significantly boost the stock’s price once Wall Street and its investors realize that it is significantly undervalued and rush to invest. The sum-of-the-parts valuation is very similar in a way, but it is used on large, complex companies that own and operate many smaller businesses under it. Wall Street often struggles to properly evaluate these large firms resulting in a conglomerate discount or an undervalued company. The sum-of-the-parts valuation system adds up all the valuations of the smaller companies under it and subtracts the corporate debt to check if the total is higher than the current stock price, at which point they buy in until a catalyst event.

#### Turnaround Investing & Special Situations

Turnaround investing relies on buying a company on the brink of bankruptcy with a realistic survival plan. The three stages of a successful turnaround starts with the company cutting all expenses, refinancing immediate debt, and choosing a new chief executive officer. The second step is to focus on operational fixes and refocus on core products at which point Wall Street will buy back in and the revenue grows again as the third step. For a company to have a chance at a turnaround, it must have no major debt due for the next couple of years and a strong core product. The three main types of special situations include corporate spin-offs, merger arbitrage, and asset liquidations. Corporate spin-offs are when a company decides to split into two separate public companies, automatically granting institutional mutual funds of the new small companies. Because the new company is too small, which means that they are breaking portfolio mandates, they are forced to sell off all of their small shares to stay legal. The stock price plummets to a very cheap point, creating a perfect entry point for an investor because the stock price plummeting does not represent the company’s health also falling. A merger arbitrage is when a company announces that it is buying another one for some price per share. The other company will trade a little bit below that price per share because there is a small risk that the government might block the merger. If the legal research shows that the merger is legal then, the risk of buying is incredibly low and nearly always guarantees the price difference in profit for an investor. Asset liquidation is when a company decides to fully shut down and sell everything it owns. The board of the company publicizes the plan for how it will sell everything and if the market cap is lower than the mathematical payout to shareholders, buying the stock will pay the shareholders the difference between the market cap and the payout.

### Growth Investing

#### Growth At a Reasonable Price (GARP) & Quality Growth

GARP investors look for companies that are growing quickly, but they refuse to overpay. They use the price-to-earnings-to-growth ratio. This metric takes the price-to-earnings ratio and divides it by the company’s earnings growth rate. A price-to-earnings-to-growth ratio of one means that a company is perfectly priced for its growth and anything under one means that the growth is discounted. This method is more useful than solely looking at the price-to-earnings ratio because if that ratio is very high, it could only be temporary while the GARP would show the full picture. Quality growth is about focusing on the business operations rather than the math. A company with potential for quality growth must have low debt, pricing power, which is a high and steady profits margin meaning that if inflation and prices spike, a quality growth company can raise its prices without losing customers, and a high return on equity. A return on equity is calculated by dividing the net income by the shareholder’s equity. Investors look for a return on equity that is around fifteen to twenty percent.

#### Compounders & Secular Growth

A compounder is a company that generates high returns on its capital and reinvest its profits back into the business. Those companies are also known as snowball businesses because their profit grows exponentially as they reinvest back into their own business. Investors find these companies by looking for high returns on invested capital, which can be calculated by dividing the company’s net operating profit after taxes by their invested capital, and a high reinvestment rate. The invested capital is calculated by the non-operating cash subtracted from the total debt plus equity. A high return on invested capital usually lies on the upper end of fifteen to twenty percent. Secular growth means a business is growing because of a massive change in the world that keeps the business moving that nothing can stop. The market does not affect these businesses because human habits or demographics have permanently shifted such as a need or want that people will always have no matter what. To identify these businesses, investors observe regular human habits to identify companies that supply those habits.

#### Hypergrowth & Momentum Growth

A hypergrowth company is a business that is expanding at an abnormal rate from about thirty to fifty percent in revenue growth. These companies are usually young trying to capture a new market. These companies usually have zero net profit because they are aggressively reinvesting to keep growing. Momentum growth is a strategy based purely on market psychology and following the trend. This strategy relies on riding waves of excitement in the market because people do not want to miss out. People begin to aggressively buy in causing the stock to keep shooting up.

#### Disruptor Investing & Innovation Investing

Disruptor investing is the strategy of investing in small companies with cheap, simple options for everyday people that evolve over time to become better and drive out the bigger and older businesses. Disruptors appear in industries that are highly inefficient and annoy customers. These businesses rely on selling to everyday people that are ignored by the larger companies, and these businesses usually have newer technology to make their product cheaper. The companies also have low net profit while having a revenue growth above thirty to fifty percent annually. Innovation investing is simply investing in a future dominator of an industry. These companies spend money to create a new product, technology, or industry. They can do this without needing to take down other old businesses, allowing them to grow without competition in their own market.

#### Growth Investing & Early-Stage Growth

Growth investing is the strategy of buying shares of a company with a high price-to-earnings ratio because the company is expected to grow their sales, revenue, and profits faster than the market expects. These companies focus on increasing their wealth through capital gains, selling an asset at a higher price than paid for it, rather than dividends, giving shareholders a piece of their earnings to decrease their stock price. They reinvest all of their money back into their own company in order to maximize growth. Early–stage growth is an extremely risky strategy relying on identifying  young startups that have a working product and a strong customer base. Many of these businesses end up failing, but the few businesses that do work out generate huge profit.

### Dividend & Income

#### Dividend Investing & Dividend Growth

Dividend investing is a straightforward strategy in which stable stocks regularly pay out a piece of their profits to investors as cash instead of needing for the stock price to shoot up just because they invested in their company. If the stock price stays relatively consistent with slow rise, it regularly earns money for the investors. Dividend growth is a strategy that focuses on buying companies that regularly increase cash payouts yearly. These companies are perfect for avoiding stock market crashes because they will still give the investors the payout.

#### Municipal Bonds & Bond Laddering

Municipal bonds are nearly riskless bonds for generating interest. By loaning the government money to use, they will pay the investor’s money with taxless interest. Bond laddering is the process of taking cash and investing it equally into bonds with differing time lengths to maximize gains with interest rates. For example, if an investor has $50,000 and invests it by putting $10,000 into a 1 year bond, $10,000 into a 2 year bond, $10,000 into a 3 year bond, $10,000 into a 4 year bond, and $10,000 into a 5 year bond, every year the investor will reinvest whichever bond paid them the money into another five year bond so that the investor can also use higher interest rates if interest rates go up and keep repeating this process.

#### Preferred Shares & Preferred Stock Investing

Preferred shares are a special type of stock that acts like a regular stock and a bond, a deal in which an investor loans a corporate or government money in exchange for an interest rate. Companies pay the investor a set amount of money a set amount of times a year before any common stockholders, and the investor gets no vote in any company decisions. It acts as a bankruptcy shield because the investor will be paid before any common stockholder. Preferred stock investing is the strategy of buying those preferred shares for cash flow, and they have lower taxes.

#### Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) Income & Infrastructure Income

A REIT is a company that owns and manages physical buildings. They pool money from all their investors to buy commercial properties. By law, they must pay investors at least ninety percent of their taxable income to investors as dividends. It earns rent  without the stress of being a physical landlord in exchange for granting some of their earnings to the company. Infrastructure income is the strategy of investing money into essential assets for people, so that they do not have any competition. Instead of standard rent, income comes from usage fees. Payouts naturally tend to rise alongside inflation because these companies can legally raise their price.

#### Covered Call Income & High-Yield Investing

Covered Call Income is an options strategy where an investor earns immediate cash by giving someone else the temporary right to buy them from the investor. There is an agreed strike price, and when the stock price hits that, the investor agrees to sell, capping maximum profit, but guaranteeing the cash premium. High-yield investing is a strategy that involves targeting stocks, bonds, or funds that pay significantly more than the market average dividend. The downside is that the stock comes with a higher principal risk.

### Quantitative Investing

#### Artificial Intelligence (AI) Investing & Machine Learning Investing

Artificial intelligence investing is a type of investing relying on artificial intelligence to execute deep research and properly tweak the portfolio accordingly. AIs can look through all of the news reports and speeches people give in seconds, but the inherent risk of hallucinations or errors still exist. The AI might misinterpret the news, and it is incredibly important that AI investors meticulously oversee the work of the AI. Machine learning investing is a much safer form of AI investing relying on pure math in order to find hidden patterns in historical stock that are solely subtle connections that are completely invisible to regular humans. The self-improving systems constantly adjust its formulas to make sharper trades in the future.

#### Quant Investing & Factor Investing

Quant investing relies completely on math, data science, and algorithms to guide decisions instead of a fund manager’s intuition. It relies on the belief that if a rule cannot be written with computer code or math, it is only superstition and wrong. These data scientists and mathematical researchers analyze the market for hidden patterns to use. Factor investing relies on picking specific traits to analyze and trading based on that. It breaks down a stock into the underlying traits in order to pick the one that has proved itself as reliable based on historical data.

#### Statistical Arbitrage & Risk Parity

Statistical arbitrage is a simple strategy that relies on mean reversion; if two statistically related stocks drift apart, they will come back to their historical average. If one of these companies drops and the other stays still, the computer that is monitoring it will buy the drop and short the one that did not change equally. Risk parity focuses on assessing how much risk each investment has and investing accordingly. If a stock is riskier, it will adjust the portfolio in order to invest less in that risky stock in order to equalize the amount of risk throughout all of the investments.

#### The Enhanced Index & Automated Portfolio Management

Creating an enhanced index relies on buying stocks from an index based on factors instead of size. A traditional S&P 500 index fund forces an investor to buy a stock solely because its price goes up and gets bigger. An enhanced index is one created by and adjusted by factors that the investors believe are most important in buying a company. Automated portfolio management is simply a machine managing a daily portfolio based on what the investor values. It automatically sells losing investments to keep a strategy on track.

#### Algorithmic Trading & High-Frequency Trading (HFT)

Algorithmic trading combines strategies and uses a code to automate a trading strategy based on programmed rules. The software continuously reads public market feeds and executes orders itself. It can be used on any timeframe making it highly accessible to all traders. High-frequency trading is a form of algorithmical trading that relies on raw speed and immense order volume. It executes millions of trades daily and relies on being faster than anyone in the market. These algorithms work at the scale of microseconds, hunting for tiny imbalances in the market. Huge firms spend massive amounts of money to be as close as possible to The World Trade Center and minimize distance for the signal to travel. They utilize the bid-ask spread, the difference between the highest price a buyer will accept and the lowest price a seller will accept in order to profit.

### Trading Styles

#### Day Trading & Swing Trading

Day trading is a style of trading where an investor completes all their trades during a single market day and never holds overnight. Day traders look to make small, frequent profits from rapid, intraday price movements. They observe minute-by-minute charts and candlestick charts to see when the buyers and buying and sellers are selling and if the volume is high. They ride the tide by guessing whether the other investors will keep buying or start selling. Swing trading is a style where traders hold trades for long times in order to capture larger movements. Swing traders look to identify the start of a price trend and ride it until it loses momentum. They look for catalyst events such as earnings reports and border market movements that could push the stock up.

#### Position Trading & Scalping

Position trading is a long-term approach where trades are held for years. By holding for years, an investor can completely ignore market noise and gain from an actually good company. Using fundamental analysis, investors look at macroeconomic trends and determine which companies have high potential. Scalping relies on executing hundreds of trades daily. Scalpers rely on compounding to earn, and they search intensively for market supply-and-demand imbalances and buy them.

#### Trend Following & Mean Reversion

Trend following relies on waiting for a clear momentum wave, and when it arrives, an investor will buy in right away. Because a stock has a higher chance of going up the next day if it went up the last, these investors will keep riding the wave until the momentum completely switches. These investors’ exit point rises with the market, locking in their gain as the stock rises, but this style yields a low win rate often around one third, but when an investor does predict it right, their gains easily wipe out their losses. Mean reversion is a strategy that relies on the belief that a stock will always come back to its baseline historical average. Investors enter when there are statistical extremes such as a relative strength index above seventy or below thirty. While this strategy does have a very high win rate, losses can be very catastrophic if a business actually collapses or explodes upwards.

#### Range Trading & Breakout Trading

Range trading relies on an investing identifying a floor, a support, and a ceiling, a resistance. Investors will be too intrigued to not buy at the support and too scared to keep buying at the resistance, using these two boundaries, range traders short and buy at the boundaries, assuming the stock will bounce in between these two. Breakout trading relies on the opposite intuition. These investors wait until range trading fails and the support or the resistance is broken. When the support breaks, they short because they believe the stock will be so low that investors will be scared and start backing out, and they buy once the resistance is passed because they believe the momentum will continue. It is a very risky strategy and has a high fail rate, but when it succeeds, it earns lots of money.

#### News Trading & Event-Driven Trading

News traders trade directly following announcements to capture rapid price adjustments. They anticipate the news and invest right before it. These traders place both buy-stop and sell-stop orders, allowing them to earn on the news whichever way it goes. Another strategy these investors use is waiting for institutional algorithms to overextend a stock’s price in the first minute, then shorting or buying the immediate counter-reaction. The biggest danger is slippage, where high-speed market volatility causes orders to execute at an unintended price. Event-driven trading is a purely mathematical form of trading where investors utilize the spread. Before a major corporate event is announced, the market rarely already has it priced at its final value and has a gap between its current value and the final value known as the spread. These investors buy the stocks from anxious shareholders who want to cash out early and sell the stock after it reaches its final price.

### Derivatives

#### Options Trading & Calendar Spreads

Option trading is the process of buying or selling contracts that give traders the right to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price within a set timeframe. The two forms of option trading are call options, bullish bets on rising prices, and put options, bearish bets on falling options. Every standard contact controls one hundred shares of the stock. Buyers pay a premium to sellers to acquire these rights. The buyers strictly limit their risk while the seller strictly cap their profit. Also, an option contract can be at-the-money (ATM), a regular fee, in-the-money (ITM), an extra discount, or out-of-the-money (OTM), a higher price in exchange for a discounted premium. A calendar spread  is a strategy where an investor buys and sells two options of the exact same type, either a call or a put, and identical strike prices, but with different expiration dates. This strategy relies on theta or time decay. Short-term options lose their value much faster than long-term options. The investor pays a net debit because long-term options are more expensive than short-term ones. The maximum risk is strictly restricted to this net debit. If the stock price stays flat, the short-term option will decay to zero, allowing the investor to keep the premium, while the long-term option retains its value. Then the investor sells the long-term option for profit.

#### Straddles & Strangles

A straddle is used when an investor expects a massive price disruption and wants the highest probability of catching the breakout when it begins. The investor buys a single ATM call option and another ATM put option. In order for this contract to work, the stock price must move more than the total premium paid. Theta will affect the straddle hard because if the stock flatlines, the contract keeps losing value because the extrinsic value will go lower as the probability of a change goes down every lost day to the expiration date. Strangles are deployed when an investor truly believes the stock to have a historical price change, but wants to risk less capital upfront than a straddle. The investor buys an OTM put and OTM call option contract. This strategy has a huge discount because an OTM option is much cheaper than an ATM option, but it has a higher chance of failure because if the stock moves and does not hit any of the far-away strike prices, the contract becomes worthless.

#### Credit Spreads & Debit Spreads

A credit spread is used when an investor believes a stock will stay flat, move in a chosen direction, or move a little against the investor without crossing a line. In order to execute this, the trader sells an option closer to the current stock price to collect a high premium and also buys another option further away from its price as insurance. This net credit collected right away represents maximum potential profit for the trade. Now, they have a ceiling on their downside risk, which is the difference between the two strike prices minus the premium. If the stock stays within its safe zone and clear of the sold strike price by expiration, both options let the investor earn fully on the credit. A debit spread is used when an investor has a strong directional conviction, but they want a substantial discount on entering the trade. Instead of buying an expensive single option, the investor buys an option closer to the stock price and simultaneously sells an option further away to offset their premium costs. Paying this net debit limits their maximum financial loss strictly to the initial amount paid. To hit maximum profitability, the underlying asset must make an aggressive price move entirely past their further sold strike price before the expiration date.

#### Butterfly Spreads & Iron Condors

A butterfly spread uses four options across three equally spaced strikes with the same expiration date. The setup is created by buying one ITM call, selling two ATM calls, and buying one OTM call. The maximum profit is generated when the stock price lands on the ATM calls’ strike price, and it is calculated by the net debit paid subtracted from the width of the strikes. An iron condor combines a bear call spread and a bull put spread. This trade is entered for a net credit. The setup of an iron condor is created by buying one OTM put, selling another OTM put closer to the stock price, selling one OTM call closer to the stock price, and buying one OTM call further away from the stock price.

#### Covered Calls & Protective Puts

A covered call is simply a quick way to generate profit. When an investor owns the stock and sells a call option against it to collect a premium, it is called a covered call. An investor agrees to lock in a maximum sale price. If the stock’s price explodes upwards, the investor misses out on those massive extra gains because their profit is capped. When an investor owns a stock and buys a put option for it is known as a protective put. Protective puts are used as insurance policies to have the ability to exit the stock at a set price no matter how far the stock falls down. The catch is that an investor has to pay a premium upfront, which lowers the overall profit if the stock keeps going up regularly.

### Short Selling

#### Short Selling & Inverse Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

Short selling is a type of investing based on profiting when the price of a stock falls. An investor, using a margin account, borrows shares from a broker, sells them immediately and tries to buy them back later at a cheaper price to return them. A short has unlimited risk because a stock’s price could rise as much as it wants past one hundred percent, completely draining the investment's value for the investor and even driving them into debt. An inverse exchange-traded fund (ETF) is when an investor buys shares of a single ETF that uses financial derivatives to move in the exact opposite direction of an index fund. An investor uses standard cash because they are simply purchasing shares of a fund, not borrowing anything, and the maximum risk is capped to the investment.

#### Long-Short Investing & Market Neutral

Long-short investing is a strategy where an investor buys stocks expected to rise, the longs, and shorts stocks expected to fall, the shorts, simultaneously. The investor puts more money into the longs because it assumes the broad market will rise over time. Market neutral is a strict style where long and short positions are perfectly balanced to cancel each other out. It maintains zero net market exposure; the total amount invested in the longs and the shorts are the exact same. This strategy makes money purely based on stock-picking skills, regardless of whether the broad stock market goes up or down.

#### Pair Trading & Bear Strategies

Pair trading is when an investor finds two highly related stocks, and waits for their usual price gap to widen. The investor simultaneously buys the stock that is underperforming and sells the stock that is overperforming, creating a profit when their prices eventually snap back to normal. This strategy allows investors to make money based on the relationship between the two stocks, regardless of the broader market. Bear strategies are simply the overall group of strategies that help an investor stay safe in a bear market. This includes buying inverse ETFs and shifting their portfolio into defensive stocks like utilities or supermarkets, which tend to hold their value even during a bear market.

## Macro

### Top-Down Investing & Bottom-Up Investing

Top-down investing starts with a broad view of the world by analyzing major economic trends, inflation, and interest rates first. Investors use this data to select the specific industries or sectors poised to grow the most in the current economy. Finally, they pick the individual stocks within those sectors that represent the industry as a whole. Bottom-up investing completely flips this process by ignoring the overall economy to focus entirely on the financial health of individual companies. Investors dive deep into a single business’s revenue, profit margins, unique products, and management quality to see if it is a worthwhile investment. The core belief is that an exceptional company with a strong competitive advantage can thrive and make money even during tough economic times.

### Currency Investing & Commodity Investing

Currency investing is the strategy of buying a country’s currency. If the country has a stronger economy than another country, the price of their currency will go up relative to the other. Interest rate arbitrage is buying currency from countries with high interest rates and selling currency from countries with low interest rates. This massive capital flight strengthens the currency of the country with the higher interest rates. During global crises, investors buy lots of the top historic economies’ currencies that have repeatedly proven to stay steady regardless of their current underlying economic data. Currency prices also dictate global trade. A country with a weaker currency has its exports become cheaper and more attractive globally, boosting its manufacturing sector but increasing the cost of its imports. Unlike stocks or bonds, commodities have no cash flow, and their value is strictly determined by the physical realities of supply constraints and consumption demands. If the world needs something that will take a long time to create, the lag will create severe supply bottlenecks, forcing prices to explode because buyers cannot wait for the supply to catch up. When central banks print money, paper fiat currencies lose purchasing power. However, assets such as crude oil, gold, or wheat act as a premier inflation hedge; their prices rise because it takes more devalued dollars to buy the same thing. Unlike stocks, most commodity investing happens via future contracts. Contango is a market condition where when future prices are higher than current prices, investors lose money every month just holding the contract. Backwardation is when demand is so desperate that spot prices are higher than future prices, so the investment becomes lucrative.

### Interest-Rate Investing & Business Cycle Investing

Interest-rate investing revolves around predicting and positioning a portfolio for central bank monetary policy shifts, such as cuts or hikes. Investors trade bonds and derivatives to capitalize on moving yields, fixed-income price changes, and shifting yield curves. Successfully timing these shifts allows market participants to maximize returns or lock in stable income before macro lending environments fundamentally change. Business cycle investing involves actively rotating capital across various economic sectors as the macroeconomy naturally fluctuates over time. Investors shift funding into industries like technology or industrials during a growth phase, then pivot to defensive industries during a downturn. This proactive reallocation tries to consistently outperform the broader market by anticipating recurring economic expansions, peaks, contractions, and troughs.

### Inflation Investing & Deflation Investing

Inflation Investing is simply investing to make sure that inflation does not cause the value of an investor’s portfolio to plummet. To keep their portfolio’s intrinsic value equal to its regular value, investors invest in Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), real estate, and commodities. The free cash flow growth must outpace this rising hurdle. Mathematically, companies with high pricing, revenue expanding faster than the consumer price index (CPI), and low capital spending, so rising input costs do not drain cash reserves. Conversely, during deflation, consumer prices collapse and interest rates drop down. Fixed overhead, business costs, and debts become even worse liabilities when interest rates drop down because the income drops, but the fixed expenses stay the exact same size. Investors must focus on companies with minimal debt to protect their net income. Because market interest rates are going down, assets with locked-in cash flow will benefit significantly because the value will be higher than it is supposed to be in a market with lower interest rates than normal.

## Alternative

### Venture Capital & Angel Investing

Angel investors are investors who use huge portions of their money, usually in the hundreds of thousands and pour it into very small companies when they have not even truly formed yet. These investors allow founders to maintain substantial autonomy and rarely demand a board seat. Because they do not have to answer to anyone, they can perform quick evaluations and invest cash within weeks. Venture capitalists are investors who are employed by a firm to manage a pooled fund of other people’s money. These investors invest a little after angel investors do when there is a working product, market traction, and customers. They raise capital from limited partners (LPs) like pension funds and foundations. The investment sizes usually are in the millions. Venture capitalists demand significant operational oversight, usually take between twenty and thirty percent equity, and mandate a seat on the board of directors. They operate on strict ten-year fund lifecycles. They will push the startup toward an aggressive exit via an acquisition or an IPO in order to return their profits to their LPs.

### Private Equity & Seed Investing

Private equity involves investment firms buying a large ownership in mature, established corporations. These investors use active management to restructure the business operations, improve financial efficiency, and maximize profitability. The ultimate goal is to hold the company for several years and then execute a lucrative exit strategy by selling it or launching an initial public offering. Seed investing is the process of providing the initial equity capital to an early-stage startup that is just beginning to develop. Founders use this critical funding to build a minimum viable product, validate market demand, and establish proof of concept. Because these companies lack an operating history, investors take on high risk in exchange for significant equity upside if the venture succeeds.

### Crowdfunding & Venture Debt

Crowdfunding lets a new company raise small amounts of money from a large group of regular people online. Startups use websites to collect these funds, granting investors early products or a small share of the business. This approach helps founders get cash quickly without needing a bank, while building a community of customers. Venture debt is a special loan given to fast-growing startups by professional lenders who understand high-tech businesses. Instead of taking away a lot of company ownership, the lender gives cash that the startup pays back with interest over time. To protect against the high risk of the startup failing, the lender also gets special options to buy stock later at a cheap price.

## Passive

### Index Investing & Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF) Investing

Index investing focuses on tracking a target market benchmark to match its overall performance rather than trying to beat it. This broad-market strategy eliminates the need to research individual corporations or time the market cycles. By maintaining a wider image of an entire market index, an investor can drastically lower the structural management costs while securing stable, long-term returns. Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) are highly flexible investment vehicles that trade continuously on a regular stock exchange throughout the business day. They pool investor cash to buy a vast basket of underlying assets, providing an investor with instant portfolio diversification in a single transaction. This structural format offers exceptional transparency, minimal transaction fees, and high liquidity for modern passive investors.

### Dollar-Cost Averaging & Buy-and-Hold

The dollar-cost averaging is the strategy of investing a fixed dollar amount on a regular, recurring schedule regardless of the market conditions. By committing a steady sum monthly, an investor automatically purchases more shares when prices peak. This mechanical strategy removes the emotional urge to time the market, effectively smoothing out an investor’s long-term average purchase costs over time. The buy-and-hold strategy is a long-term investment philosophy centered around keeping assets through all market noise, expansions, and contractions. This approach is rooted in the historical reality that broad market indices trend upward over multi-decade periods. By maintaining strict portfolio stability and refusing to panic-sell during crashes, an investor minimizes costly trading fees, lowers their taxes, and fully uses the power of compounding.

# Merger Arbitrage

*Family: `advanced` · id: `merger_arb`*

## Philosophy
Merger arbitrage buys the target stock after a deal is announced, earning
the spread to the offer price if the transaction closes. You are selling
insurance against deal break — compensated for regulatory risk, financing
failure, shareholder vote loss, and macro shocks that kill M&A appetite.
Philosophy: repeat many small positive spreads with rigorous break
probability analysis beats betting on one heroic deal.

This is not risk-free: broken deals can gap down 30–50%. Position sizing
and diversification across uncorrelated deals are core to survival.

## How it works
Workflow: screen announced deals → compute gross spread and annualized
return → estimate break probability from precedent, antitrust, financing,
vote math → size inversely to risk → monitor milestones (HSR, proxy, close
date). Stock-for-stock deals may require hedge on acquirer.

Types: cash deals (simpler), stock deals (hedge ratio), hostile (higher
break risk), strategic vs financial buyer. Legal timeline drives IRR —
delayed close compresses annualized return but may widen spread opportunity.

## Formulas
### Merger spread
$$Spread = \frac{Deal\ Price - P}{P}$$
Gross profit if deal closes at stated terms.

### Annualized spread
$$Ann = \left(1 + Spread\right)^{365/days} - 1$$
Compare deals on time-adjusted basis.

### Expected return
$$E[R] = p_{close} \cdot Spread + (1-p_{close}) \cdot Loss_{break}$$
Probability-weighted outcome — heart of risk arb.

## Screens / checklist
- Spread compensates for estimated break risk
- Antitrust path plausible (no direct overlap red flag)
- Financing committed for LBO
- Vote threshold achievable
- Diversify ≥ 8–15 deals

## Traps & failure modes
- Concentration in one break wiping year
- Ignoring material adverse change clauses
- Stock deal acquirer collapse unhedged
- Regulatory surprise (FTC block)

## Catalysts
- Proxy approval
- regulatory clearance
- early close

# Convertible Arbitrage

*Family: `advanced` · id: `convertible_arb`*

## Philosophy
Convertible arbitrage buys convertible bonds (debt + embedded equity option)
and hedges the equity delta by shorting common stock — harvesting volatility,
credit spread, and cheap optionality. Philosophy: extract mispriced convexity
while staying roughly neutral to small stock moves. Requires dynamic hedging:
as stock rises, delta rises → short more; as stock falls, cover shorts.

A specialist strategy — borrow, corporate credit, and vol modeling must be
world-class. Retail access limited; concept still educates on bond-equity
linkage and gamma.

## How it works
Buy convert → compute delta from option model → short stock to neutralize
→ earn coupon minus borrow cost plus trading gamma (profit from hedging
stock swings). Credit hedge optional (CDS) if issuer fragile. Catalysts:
repricing on earnings, takeout premium, vol crush.

Risks: credit event (stock gaps down through hedge), hard borrow, vol
collapse hurting option value. Portfolio many small positions diversifies
issuer risk.

## Formulas
### Convert delta hedge
$$Shares_{short} = \Delta \times \frac{Par}{Conversion\ ratio}$$
Neutralize small moves in underlying.

### Conversion parity
$$Parity = \frac{Stock \times Par}{Conversion\ price}$$
Bond price vs equity-linked fair value.

### Gamma P&L (concept)
$$P\&L_{\gamma} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma (\Delta S)^2$$
Profit from re-hedging as stock moves.

## Screens / checklist
- Cheap vol vs realized history
- Credit spread compensates default risk
- Borrow available on hedge leg
- Liquidity in convert and stock
- Issuer diversification

## Traps & failure modes
- Credit blow-up (stock down, convert down, short wins insufficient)
- Gamma scalping costs exceed edge
- Forced short cover in squeeze
- Illiquid converts mark stale

## Catalysts
- Vol expansion
- takeout bid
- credit upgrade

# Volatility Trading

*Family: `advanced` · id: `volatility_trading`*

## Philosophy
Volatility trading expresses views on realized vs implied volatility — not
direction per se. Options imply a breakeven move (IV); if realized vol is
lower, systematic sellers collect premium; if shocks arrive, long vol pays.
Philosophy: vol is mean-reverting but fat-tailed — short vol earns steady
small gains interrupted by rare large losses (volmageddon).

Understand VIX as sentiment gauge, not investable asset long term. Position
size for tail risk when short; define max loss when long (theta bleed).

## How it works
Strategies: (1) short strangles/iron condors when IV rich vs forecast,
(2) long straddles into events when IV cheap vs expected move, (3) variance
swaps at institutional level, (4) VIX futures term structure trades
(contango roll down). Hedge with spreads to cap tail.

Monitor skew, term structure, and correlation. Earnings: compare implied
move to historical realized. Risk: gap opens blow short vol; long vol bleeds
in calm summers.

## Formulas
### Implied vs realized
$$Edge \propto IV - RV_{forecast}$$
Sell when IV overstates expected realized; buy when understated.

### Straddle breakeven
$$BE_{\uparrow} = S_0 + Premium, \quad BE_{\downarrow} = S_0 - Premium$$
Underlying must move beyond total premium paid.

### Vega exposure
$$\Delta P \approx \mathcal{V} \cdot \Delta \sigma$$
Option value sensitivity to implied vol change.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.options`

## Screens / checklist
- IV percentile vs 1-year range
- Event implied move vs historical earnings move
- Position defined-risk (spreads not naked)
- Margin stress test on +10 VIX points
- Theta budget for long vol holds

## Traps & failure modes
- Short vol unlimited tail (Feb 2018)
- Long vol theta decay in quiet markets
- Misreading VIX futures roll cost
- Over-leveraged iron condors at lows

## Catalysts
- CPI/FOMC surprise
- geopolitical gap
- earnings shock

# Distressed Debt

*Family: `advanced` · id: `distressed_debt`*

## Philosophy
Distressed debt buys bonds, loans, or claims of troubled issuers trading
well below par, betting on recovery via restructuring, asset sales, or
improved operations. Philosophy: bankruptcy is a process, not an endpoint —
legal rules create claims hierarchy (secured > unsecured > equity) and
negotiation windows where informed capital earns outsized returns. Requires
credit, legal, and game-theory fluency.

Losses are real when recovery is zero; diversification and seniority matter.
Public market proxy: HY spreads widening, CCC bucket, post-reorg equities.

## How it works
Screen 13D/8-K, missed coupons, exchange offers, Ch. 11 filings. Model
enterprise value scenarios → allocate to debt tranches → compare to bond
price. Trade bank debt, bonds, or post-reorg equity (GORO). Fulcrum security
converts to equity in reorg.

Timeline: months to years in court. Liquidity dries — position sizing for
hold. Catalysts: DIP financing, 363 sale, plan confirmation.

## Formulas
### Recovery rate
$$Recovery = \frac{Proceeds\ to\ tranche}{Face\ value}$$
Central underwriting variable.

### Yield to maturity (distressed)
$$YTM \approx \frac{Coupon + (Par - P)/years}{P}$$
Simplified current yield on deep discount.

### Fulcrum concept
$$Tranche\ where\ EV \approx Claim\ amount$$
Often becomes equity in restructuring.

## Screens / checklist
- Seniority appropriate for scenario
- Collateral coverage in liquidation case
- Legal counsel quality on restructuring
- Liquidity for exit or hold to plan
- Issuer industry salvageable demand

## Traps & failure modes
- Buying subordinated with no recovery
- Fighting secured lenders in court without resources
- Illiquidity trapping capital
- Equitization wipe of junior debt

## Catalysts
- Plan confirmation
- 363 asset sale
- economic upturn lifting EV

# Leveraged Investing

*Family: `advanced` · id: `leveraged_investing`*

## Philosophy
Leverage amplifies exposure via margin, derivatives, or leveraged ETFs —
magnifying gains and losses and introducing path dependency. Philosophy:
leverage is a tool, not a strategy; use only when edge and risk management
are explicit. Borrowing at rate r to earn return R works when R > r
consistently, but volatility triggers margin calls before long-run averages
materialize.

Kelly and fractional Kelly frame sizing; most investors should use far
less than theoretical optimum.

## How it works
Channels: brokerage margin (Reg T 50% initial), portfolio margin for
sophisticates, futures notional, LEAPS, 2×/3× ETFs (daily reset). Monitor
maintenance margin, interest expense, and drawdown triggers.

De-lever before forced liquidation. Pair leverage with hedges or strict
stops. LBO is corporate leverage; personal leverage is personal bankruptcy
risk.

## Formulas
### Leveraged return
$$R_{lev} \approx LR \cdot R_{asset} - (L-1) \cdot r_{borrow}$$
L = leverage multiple; borrow cost drags.

### Margin call threshold
$$Equity\% = \frac{Assets - Debt}{Assets} < maintenance \Rightarrow call$$
Forced sale at worst prices.

### Kelly fraction
$$f^* = \frac{p \cdot b - q}{b}$$
Theoretical optimal bet size; use fraction in practice.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.quant.kelly_fraction`

## Screens / checklist
- Leverage ratio within written policy max
- Borrow rate and margin interest known
- Stress test −30% asset move survivable
- No leverage on illiquid holdings
- Emergency liquidity reserve outside margin

## Traps & failure modes
- Margin call cascade
- Leveraged ETF decay in chop
- Concentrated bet + leverage = ruin
- Ignoring correlation in portfolio margin

## Catalysts
- Vol collapse enabling cheaper leverage
- refinance lower borrow rate

# Tactical Asset Allocation

*Family: `advanced` · id: `tactical_aa`*

## Philosophy
Tactical asset allocation (TAA) shifts portfolio weights among stocks, bonds,
cash, and alternatives based on short- to medium-term outlooks — deviating

TAA is not market timing heroics — it's risk management when valuations,

## Philosophy

# Portfolio Optimization

*Family: `advanced` · id: `portfolio_optimization`*

## Philosophy
Portfolio optimization solves for weights maximizing risk-adjusted return
subject to constraints — classic Markowitz mean-variance and extensions
(risk parity, Black-Litterman, robust optimization). Philosophy: inputs
dominate outputs; expected returns are estimated badly, so optimizers
chase noise (error maximization). Use optimization for discipline and
risk budgeting, not oracle weights.

Shrinkage covariances, resampling, and constraints (max weight, sector caps)
make solutions usable.

## How it works
Estimate μ (returns), Σ (covariance) from history or models → solve
max Sharpe or min variance → apply constraints → implement → monitor drift.
Black-Litterman blends equilibrium returns with investor views. Risk parity
equalizes risk contributions instead of dollars.

Backtest out-of-sample; turnover and costs in objective. FATE uses optimization
concepts in fortress risk budgeting.

## Formulas
### Mean-variance objective
$$\max_w \frac{w^T \mu - r_f}{\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}}$$
Maximize Sharpe ratio portfolio.

### Minimum variance
$$\min_w w^T \Sigma w \quad s.t. \quad \sum w_i = 1$$
Global minimum variance portfolio.

### Sharpe ratio
$$Sharpe = \frac{E[R_p - r_f]}{\sigma_p}$$
Objective function scalar.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.risk.sharpe_ratio`

## Screens / checklist
- Covariance matrix shrinkage applied
- Max single-name weight cap
- Out-of-sample Sharpe > in-sample sanity check
- Turnover penalty in optimization
- Constraints match mandate (long-only, sector)

## Traps & failure modes
- Error maximization from noisy μ
- Ill-conditioned covariance matrix
- Look-ahead bias in backtest
- Ignoring transaction costs

## Catalysts
- New asset class addition
- correlation regime shift

# Sector Rotation

*Family: `advanced` · id: `sector_rotation`*

## Philosophy
Sector rotation overweights sectors expected to lead the next cycle phase
and underweights laggards — connecting macro, earnings breadth, and relative
strength. Philosophy: sectors are the right granularity between macro and
stock picking for many investors. Early cycle: financials, industrials;
late cycle: staples, healthcare; recession: utilities, Treasuries.

Timing is imperfect; partial rotation beats all-in-all-out whipsaw. The
investment clock is a map, not GPS — arrive late to a rotation and you
still may capture the middle of a phase. Avoid doubling up: if macro
already max-long cyclicals, sector rotation should not stack the same bet
without acknowledging risk budget.

## How it works

via sector ETFs (XLF, XLE, etc.) or stock screens within sectors.

Rebalance quarterly or on phase change. Cap sector concentration for risk.
Combine with bottom-up picks within leading sectors. Build a composite score:
weight RS 40%, earnings revision breadth 30%, macro phase fit 30%. Require
two consecutive months of signal agreement before full tilt to reduce
whipsaw. Track implementation shortfall — sector ETFs gap on rebalances;
use limit orders or staged trades. After rotation, measure attribution:
did the sector sleeve add value vs static benchmark?

- Max sector overweight defined (e.g. +10% vs benchmark)
- Transaction costs vs tilt magnitude

## Traps & failure modes
- Rotating after move completed
- Single indicator false signal
- Ignoring stock-specific risk within sector
- Tax drag in taxable accounts

# Multi-Asset Investing

*Family: `advanced` · id: `multi_asset`*

## Philosophy
Multi-asset investing combines equities, bonds, commodities, alternatives,
and cash in one risk-managed portfolio — seeking diversification across
drivers of return. Philosophy: correlations are unstable; diversification
works best when painful (bonds hedge equities until inflation shock). Stress
test assumptions; include assets that behave differently in crises.

Endowment model popularized illiquid alts; retail multi-asset uses liquid
ETFs and funds with simpler governance.

## How it works
Define risk budget (vol target 10%). Allocate to sleeves with low historical
correlation. Rebalance. Use risk parity or strategic weights. Monitor
correlation breakdown — 2008 and 2022 taught bonds don't always hedge.

Alternatives sleeve: REITs, gold, managed futures, private equity funds
of funds. Liquidity ladder: cash for 1 year, bonds 1–5, equities long.

## Formulas
### Portfolio variance
$$\sigma_p^2 = w^T \Sigma w$$
Diversification reduces σ if correlations < 1.

### Correlation breakdown stress
$$\rho_{equity,bond} \rightarrow 1 \text{ in liquidity crisis}$$
Motivation for tail hedges beyond bonds.

## Screens / checklist
- ≥ 4 asset classes represented
- Liquidity matches spending needs
- Stress test 2008 and 2022 paths
- Alternative sleeve sized for illiquidity
- Rebalance policy documented

# Bonds

*Family: `asset_class` · id: `bonds`*

## Philosophy
Bonds are contractual claims on fixed or floating cash flows — ballast for portfolios when equities sell off. Return drivers: coupon income, price change from yield moves, and credit spread dynamics. Duration is the dominant risk: long bonds amplify rate sensitivity.

## How it works
Price moves inversely to yield. Investment-grade corporates add spread over Treasuries; high yield trades equity-like in stress. Ladder maturities to manage reinvestment risk. Real return = nominal minus inflation; TIPS link to CPI.

## Formulas

$$CY = \frac{\text{Annual coupon}}{P}$$

- Credit quality appropriate to risk budget
- Positive real yield after inflation

- Illiquid back-month contracts

## Catalysts

# Real Estate Investing

*Family: `real_assets` · id: `real_estate`*

## Philosophy
Direct real estate ownership generates rental income and potential
appreciation while offering inflation-linked cash flows and tangible
collateral. Unlike stocks, properties are illiquid, lumpy, and operationally
intensive — tenants, capex, zoning, and leverage dominate outcomes. Philosophy:
location and entry cap rate set the foundation; operations and financing
determine whether you compound or bleed.

Real estate diversifies portfolios with low correlation to equities when
leverage is moderate. Public access via REITs trades liquidity for tax
complexity of direct ownership.

## How it works
Analyze market rent, vacancy, expenses, and capex → derive NOI → cap rate
= NOI / price → compare to cost of debt → DSCR for lender comfort. Value-add:
renovate to raise rents; core: stable yield; opportunistic: development.

Financing: fixed-rate mortgages lock cost; floating adds risk. Hold period
5–10+ years typical. Exit: sell, 1031 exchange (US), or refinance and
distribute. REITs offer sector tilts without landlord labor.

## Formulas
### Cap rate
$$Cap = \frac{NOI}{Property\ Value}$$
Unlevered yield; lower cap = higher price per income dollar.

### NOI
$$NOI = Gross\ Rent - Operating\ Expenses$$
Before debt service and taxes.

### Cash-on-cash return
$$CoC = \frac{Annual\ Cash\ Flow\ after\ debt}{Equity\ invested}$$
Levered yield to equity investors.

### DSCR
$$DSCR = \frac{NOI}{Debt\ Service}$$
Lenders often require > 1.2–1.25.

## Screens / checklist
- Cap rate vs market comps and debt cost spread positive
- Vacancy and rent growth assumptions conservative
- Inspection and environmental clean
- DSCR comfortable in stress case
- Property management plan (self vs pro)

## Traps & failure modes
- Over-leverage in rising rate environment
- Deferred maintenance capex surprise
- Rent control / regulatory shock
- Illiquidity forcing distressed sale

## Catalysts
- Rate cuts compressing cap rates
- zoning up-density
- infrastructure improving location

# Infrastructure Real Assets

*Family: `real_assets` · id: `infrastructure_assets`*

## Philosophy
Infrastructure — toll roads, airports, regulated utilities, pipelines,
fiber networks — generates long-lived cash flows often inflation-linked or
regulated. Philosophy: bond-like equity with GDP-linked upside. Listed
infrastructure (MLPs, infra funds) offers liquidity; direct/project finance
offers yield but construction and political risk.

Assets sit between pure real estate and utilities: contracted revenues,
high barriers to entry, ESG scrutiny on fossil pipelines vs renewables.
Infrastructure often trades on yield spread to Treasuries — when rates
rise, prices fall even when contracted cash flows are stable. Match horizon
to concession life: a 30-year asset in a 5-year mandate is a mismatch.

## How it works
Models: availability-based (fixed payments) vs demand-based (volume risk).
Underwrite concession length, tariff escalators, maintenance capex, and
refinancing. Listed proxies: Brookfield Infrastructure, utility hybrids.

Political risk: renegotiation of tolls, permit delays. Green transition
favors grid, renewables transmission, data-center power. MLPs pass through
K-1 tax complexity. Stress-test volume declines on toll roads and throughput
on pipelines. Read regulatory filings for allowed ROE and rate-case calendars.
Diversify across regulated utilities, user-pay transport, and digital infra
(cell towers, fiber) so one policy shock does not dominate. Compare dividend
yield plus growth to corporate bond yields in same rating bucket.

Lifecycle cash flows including construction and ops.

### Dividend yield (listed)
$$Yield = \frac{Annual\ DPS}{Price}$$
Income component for infra equities.

### Inflation linker
$$Tariff_t = Tariff_0 \times (1 + CPI)^t$$
Contractual escalators protect real returns.

## Screens / checklist
- Concession or regulation length ≥ 15 years
- Maintenance capex funded
- Political jurisdiction stable
- ESG alignment with mandate
- Debt/EBITDA within covenants

## Traps & failure modes

- Regulatory clawback
- MLP tax complexity for holders
- Construction overrun on project finance

## Catalysts

## Philosophy

$$Gold \uparrow \text{ when } r_{real} \downarrow,\quad r_{real} = r_{nominal} - \pi^e$$
Opportunity cost framework.

## How it works
Henry Hub (US) vs TTF (Europe) vs JKM (Asia) benchmarks diverge with infrastructure. Winter heating and summer cooling drive seasonal spikes; LNG liquefaction adds global arbitrage. For equities, analyze breakeven costs, hedging books, pipeline contract mix (fee-based vs commodity-exposed), and leverage through the price cycle. Mature producers trade on P/E and FCF yield at mid-cycle gas; growth LNG names on project NPV and take-or-pay contracts. Screen for balance-sheet resilience, capital discipline, and peer-relative cost curves.

## Formulas
### Storage vs 5yr avg
$$\Delta S = S_{now} - S_{5yr}$$
Inventory surplus/deficit drives seasonal pricing.

### Spark spread
$$SS = P_{power} - \frac{P_{gas}}{HR}$$
Gas plant profitability: power price minus gas cost.

# Fundamental Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `fundamental`*

## Philosophy
Fundamental analysis examines financial statements, business models, and
competitive position to estimate intrinsic worth — asking what a business is
truly worth independent of daily price noise. Philosophy: price is what you
pay, value is what you get. Markets misprice when complexity, neglect, or
emotion obscure cash-flow reality. Fundamentals anchor long horizons; they
do not guarantee short-term performance.

## How it works
Pipeline: understand business → analyze statements → forecast drivers →
value (DCF, comps) → compare to price → monitor thesis KPIs. Cross-check
accruals vs cash flow, capital allocation, and industry structure.

FATE `analytics.value_investing` automates core screens; human judgment
handles moat and management qualitative overlay.

## Formulas
### Free cash flow
$$FCF = CFO - CapEx$$
Cash available after maintaining the business.

### Intrinsic value
$$IV = \sum \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{TV}{(1+r)^n}$$
DCF anchor for fundamentals.
*Implemented:* `analytics.value_investing.intrinsic_value`

## Screens / checklist
- Revenue and margin drivers understood
- FCF positive or credible path
- Balance sheet supports downturn
- Valuation vs history and peers
- Thesis KPIs tracked quarterly

## Traps & failure modes
- Narrative without numbers
- Peak-cycle earnings as normal
- Ignoring dilution and stock comp
- Complexity without edge

## Catalysts
- Earnings validating thesis
- multiple expansion on proof

Live `analytics/value_investing.py` — intrinsic value, deep value, Graham, Buffett scores.

`valuation`, `financial_statement`, `dcf`, `bottom_up`

# Technical Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `technical`*

## Philosophy
Technical analysis studies price, volume, and pattern history to forecast
short- to medium-term direction — based on the idea that collective behavior
leaves footprints in charts. Philosophy: timing and risk management, not
replacement for fundamentals on multi-year holds. All information is in the
price is half-true; sentiment and liquidity show up before filings.

Use indicators as context, not oracle. Combine with stops and position sizing.

## How it works
Tools: trend (moving averages, MACD), momentum (RSI), volatility (Bollinger),
structure (support/resistance, breakouts). Volume confirms moves. Multi-timeframe
analysis aligns daily entries with weekly trend.

Backtest patterns with realistic costs. FATE uses trend_ema_macd and structure
patterns in hedge fund stack for soft signals.

## Formulas
### RSI
$$RSI = 100 - \frac{100}{1 + RS}, \quad RS = \frac{Avg\ gain}{Avg\ loss}$$
Overbought >70, oversold <30 — context not command.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.technical.rsi`

### MACD
$$MACD = EMA_{12} - EMA_{26}$$
Trend momentum crossover signal.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.technical.macd`

### Mean reversion z-score
$$z = \frac{P - \mu}{\sigma}$$
Stretch from moving average.
*Implemented:* `signals.dip_momentum`

## Screens / checklist
- Trend direction defined on higher timeframe
- Volume confirms breakout
- Risk/reward ≥ 2:1 at entry
- Stop level placed before entry
- Not fighting strong fundamental deterioration

## Traps & failure modes
- Indicator overload conflicting signals
- Curve-fitted patterns
- Ignoring gap risk overnight
- Trading illiquid names on chart alone

## Catalysts
- Breakout on volume
- oversold bounce in uptrend

## In FATE_AlgoBot

## Philosophy
Quantitative analysis replaces discretionary judgment with statistical models,
factor tests, and reproducible signals — demanding rigorous validation and
humility about overfitting. Philosophy: measure everything testable; invest

behind quant investing execution, not a black box to trust blindly.

# Valuation Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `valuation`*

## Philosophy
Valuation analysis converts forecasts into price estimates — triangulating
DCF, trading comps, precedent transactions, and asset-based methods.
Philosophy: valuation is a range, not a point; assumptions dominate outputs.
Conservative inputs and multiple methods beat false precision. Margin of
safety closes the gap between estimate and purchase price.

Growth, risk, and reinvestment must be internally consistent — you cannot
assume high growth, high margins, and low risk simultaneously without moat
justification.

## How it works
Build driver-based model (revenue, margin, capex, WC, tax) → DCF with
sensitivity table → cross-check EV/EBITDA vs peers → optional SOTP for

Document bear/base/bull. Update when drivers change, not when price annoys you.

## Formulas
### DCF intrinsic value
$$IV = \sum \frac{FCF_t}{(1+WACC)^t} + \frac{TV}{(1+WACC)^n}$$
Enterprise DCF; equity = EV − net debt.
*Implemented:* `analytics.value_investing.intrinsic_value`

### EV/EBITDA
$$\frac{EV}{EBITDA}$$
Primary trading multiple for many industries.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.analysis.ev_ebitda`

### Margin of safety
$$MOS = \frac{IV - P}{IV}$$
Purchase discipline.
*Implemented:* `analytics.value_investing.margin_of_safety`

### WACC
$$WACC = \frac{E}{V}r_e + \frac{D}{V}r_d(1-T)$$
Discount rate for DCF.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.analysis.wacc`

## Screens / checklist
- Triangulation ≥ 2 methods within reasonable spread
- Sensitivity on WACC and terminal growth
- Peer comps truly comparable
- Terminal value < 70% of EV unless justified
- MOS hurdle met for buys

## Traps & failure modes
- Hockey-stick terminal growth
- Comps from bubble peers
- Ignoring net debt and dilution
- Circular logic (using price to justify price)

## Catalysts

# Risk Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `risk`*

## Philosophy
Risk analysis quantifies volatility, drawdown, factor exposures, and tail
scenarios for sizing and hedging — asking whether return compensates for
risk taken. Philosophy: return without risk context is meaningless; survival
enables compounding. Risk is multidimensional: market beta, credit, liquidity,
concentration, and model risk differ.

VaR and Sharpe are tools, not answers. Stress tests and scenario analysis
capture fat tails mean-variance misses.

## How it works
Estimate beta, vol, correlation matrix. Compute Sharpe, Sortino, max drawdown.
Decompose factor exposures (Fama-French, momentum). Run scenarios (rates +200bp,
recession EPS −30%). Set position limits from worst-case loss tolerance.

FATE `investing.formulas.risk` implements CAPM beta and Sharpe; fortress
enforces live risk budgets.

## Formulas
### CAPM beta
$$\beta_i = \frac{Cov(R_i, R_m)}{Var(R_m)}$$
Market sensitivity for hedging and expected return.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.risk.capm_beta`

### Sharpe ratio
$$Sharpe = \frac{E[R - r_f]}{\sigma}$$
Return per unit total risk.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.risk.sharpe_ratio`

### Max drawdown
$$MDD = \max_t \frac{Peak_t - Value_t}{Peak_t}$$
Worst peak-to-trough loss path.

### Value at Risk (concept)
$$VaR_\alpha = \inf\{l : P(Loss \leq l) \geq \alpha\}$$
Loss threshold at confidence α — know limitations.

## Screens / checklist
- Portfolio beta within mandate
- Max single-name weight cap
- Stress loss < survivability threshold
- Liquidity days-to-exit estimated
- Factor crowding monitored

## Traps & failure modes
- VaR false comfort in fat tails
- Historical vol underestimating regime shift
- Ignoring correlation spike in crisis
- Leverage hidden in derivatives

## Catalysts
- Vol regime drop enabling size increase
- hedge payoff in stress

# Monte Carlo Simulation

*Family: `analysis` · id: `monte_carlo`*

## Philosophy
Monte Carlo simulation generates thousands of random paths for returns,
prices, or portfolio values to estimate probability distributions — communicating
uncertainty better than single-point DCF outputs. Philosophy: inputs are
distributions, not false-precision scalars; output is a range of outcomes
and percentiles (P10, P50, P90), not one fair value.

GBM is a starting point, not reality — fat tails and correlation breaks
require stress overlays beyond vanilla Monte Carlo.

## How it works
Specify distributions for drivers (growth μ, σ; margins; rates). Draw random
samples → compute N path valuations or portfolio returns → histogram outcomes
→ read percentile MOS. FATE `monte_carlo_mean_paths` in investing.formulas.scenario
implements GBM terminal mean for teaching demos.

Use for: DCF sensitivity, retirement success probability, option path P&L.
Seed RNG for reproducibility in research.

## Formulas
### GBM path
$$S_{t+1} = S_t \exp\left((\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\right)$$
Geometric Brownian motion step; Z ~ N(0,1).
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.analysis.monte_carlo_mean_paths`

### Percentile value
$$V_{P_k} = \text{kth percentile of simulated } V_T$$
e.g. P10 bear, P50 median, P90 bull.

### Probability of loss
$$P(V_T < P_0) = \frac{\#\{paths < P_0\}}{N}$$
Risk metric for position sizing.

## Screens / checklist
- Input distributions justified (not arbitrary)
- ≥ 10,000 paths for stable percentiles
- Correlated variables use copula/Cholesky where needed
- Fat-tail stress scenario alongside MC
- Results compared to analytical DCF

## Traps & failure modes
- Garbage μ,σ producing pretty charts
- Independence assumption across years
- Ignoring path dependency of leverage
- Overconfidence from P50 alone

## Catalysts
- Narrowing distribution as uncertainty resolves

# DCF Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `dcf`*

## Philosophy
DCF analysis discounts projected free cash flows plus terminal value to
derive enterprise and equity value — the bridge from business drivers to
fair price. Philosophy: small changes in WACC or terminal growth swing outputs
wildly; therefore sensitivity tables and conservative assumptions matter more
than spreadsheet decimals. DCF is mandatory thinking even when you ultimately
decide via comps.

Terminal value often dominates — stress it as perpetuity growth, exit multiple,
or both.

## How it works
Forecast explicit period (5–10y) FCF from revenue, margin, tax, capex, WC.
Discount at WACC. Terminal: Gordon g or exit EV/EBITDA. Enterprise value −
net debt = equity ÷ shares = per-share IV. Compare to price; build sensitivity
grid on WACC × g.

with Yahoo FCF feeds.

## Formulas
### DCF
$$EV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{FCF_t}{(1+WACC)^t} + \frac{TV}{(1+WACC)^n}$$
Enterprise value from discounted flows.
*Implemented:* `analytics.value_investing.intrinsic_value`

### Terminal value (Gordon)
$$TV = \frac{FCF_n(1+g)}{WACC - g}$$
Requires g < WACC.
*Implemented:* `analytics.value_investing.terminal_value`

### Equity value
$$E = EV - NetDebt + NonOpAssets$$
Bridge to per-share value.

## Screens / checklist
- FCF forecast tied to explicit drivers
- WACC defensible (beta, ERP, credit spread)
- g ≤ GDP-like in terminal
- TV sensitivity table completed
- Cross-check EV/EBITDA implied by TV

## Traps & failure modes
- g ≥ WACC
- Peak margins forever
- Ignoring SBC dilution
- TV > 80% with heroic g

## Catalysts
- Market convergence to DCF fair value
- FCF beat raising IV

# WACC Estimation

*Family: `analysis` · id: `wacc`*

## Philosophy
Weighted average cost of capital blends cost of equity and after-tax cost of
debt to discount future cash flows — the hurdle rate for creating value.
Philosophy: WACC disagreement drives most DCF arguments; beta, equity risk
premium, and credit spread estimates are judgment calls with wide error bands.
Use consistent methodology across comparables.

Cost of equity via CAPM is common; practitioners add size and country premia
for international or small-cap names.

## How it works
Cost of equity r_e = r_f + β × ERP (adjust for leverage if unlevering beta).
Cost of debt r_d from yield or rating spread, tax-adjusted. Weights E/V and
D/V from market values. Plug into WACC formula → discount FCF.

Sensitivity: ±1% WACC can move IV 15–25% on typical models. Document every
input source.

## Formulas
### WACC
$$WACC = \frac{E}{V}r_e + \frac{D}{V}r_d(1-T)$$
Standard weighted average formula.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.analysis.wacc`

### CAPM cost of equity
$$r_e = r_f + \beta (ERP)$$
Beta from regression vs index; ERP ~5–7% historical debate.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.risk.capm_beta`

### Unlevered beta
$$\beta_u = \frac{\beta_l}{1 + (1-T)\frac{D}{E}}$$
Relever for target capital structure.

## Screens / checklist
- Beta estimated with 2–5y weekly returns
- ERP consistent with company country
- Debt cost from market yield not book coupon
- Market value weights not book
- Sensitivity table on r_e and WACC

## Traps & failure modes
- Using book D/E weights
- Beta from thin trading or wrong index
- Ignoring operating leases as debt
- Vanity low WACC inflating IV

## Catalysts
- Credit upgrade lowering r_d
- beta fall as company matures

# Comparable Company Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `comps`*

## Philosophy
Comparable company analysis values a firm using trading multiples of similar
public peers — EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Sales — adjusting for growth, margins,
risk, and scale. Philosophy: the market's current pricing of peers is a
reality check on your DCF; comps fail when peers are not truly comparable
or when the whole sector is in a bubble.

Normalize EBITDA, use forward metrics for growth names, and document why
your target deserves premium/discount to median.

## How it works
Select 5–10 peers same industry, size band, geography. Compute multiples for
each; take median/mean. Apply to target metric → implied EV or price. Triangulate
EV/EBITDA, P/E, PEG. Adjust for net debt to equity value.

FATE `ev_ebitda` and comps_implied_price in investing.formulas.analysis.

## Formulas
### EV/EBITDA
$$\frac{EV}{EBITDA}$$
Capital-structure-neutral multiple; primary for industrials.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.analysis.ev_ebitda`

### Implied price
$$P_{implied} = Multiple_{peer} \times Metric_{per\ share}$$
e.g. peer P/E × target EPS.
*Implemented:* `investing.formulas.analysis.comps_implied_price`

### PEG ratio
$$PEG = \frac{P/E}{g}$$
Growth-adjusted multiple; g in percent.

## Screens / checklist
- Peers truly comparable business model
- Use LTM and NTM metrics consistently
- Adjust for one-time items in EBITDA
- Document premium/discount rationale
- Cross-check with DCF not far divergent

## Traps & failure modes
- Bubble comps implying bubble valuation
- Different accounting standards across peers
- Negative EBITDA forcing P/S only
- Ignoring net debt in EV bridge

## Catalysts
- Peer re-rating dragging target
- sector multiple expansion

# Precedent Transaction Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `precedent_transactions`*

## Philosophy
Precedent transaction analysis benchmarks value using multiples paid in recent
M&A deals for similar targets — capturing control premium and synergy
expectations that trading comps omit. Philosophy: strategics pay more than
market; financial sponsors pay LBO math. Use precedents for takeout valuation
and merger arb ceiling, not going-concern minority stakes without catalyst.

Deal multiples include synergies buyers expect — adjust down for minority.

## How it works
Screen deals last 3–5 years same industry, size, geography. Collect EV/EBITDA,
EV/Revenue at announcement. Compute median; apply to target. Note strategic
vs sponsor, competitive auction vs negotiated, regulatory conditions.

Compare to trading comps premium — typical control premium 20–40% depending
sector. Update for current rate environment (LBO multiples compress with rates).

## Formulas
### Control premium
$$Premium = \frac{Deal - Price_{pre}}{Price_{pre}}$$
Measure over undisturbed price.

### Transaction EV/EBITDA
$$\frac{EV_{deal}}{EBITDA_{LTM}}$$
Deal multiple including synergies priced in.

## Screens / checklist
- Deals < 3 years old unless structural change
- Target size within 0.5–2× revenue scale
- Exclude distressed fire sales from median
- Synergy narrative assessed for overpayment
- Regulatory precedent for antitrust clearance

## Traps & failure modes
- One outlier deal skewing median
- Different accounting EBITDA definitions
- Failed deals included
- Applying 2021 multiples in 2026 rate regime

## Catalysts
- Strategic review announcement
- activist pushing sale
- industry consolidation wave

# Economic Moat Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `economic_moat`*

## Philosophy
Economic moat analysis identifies durable competitive advantages that protect
returns on capital from competition and commoditization. Philosophy: without
a moat, growth investments destroy value as competitors arbitrage profits;
with a moat, reinvestment compounds at high ROIC for years. Moats are not
marketing slogans — they must show up in stable margins and ROIC > WACC
through cycles.

Moats can erode: technology shifts, regulation, or management mistakes
breach once-wide castles.

## How it works
Moat sources checklist (Morningstar/Dorsey framework):
(1) Network effects — value rises with users (payments, platforms).
(2) Switching costs — painful to leave (ERP, bank relationships).
(3) Intangible assets — brand, patents, licenses.
(4) Cost advantage — scale, process, location.
(5) Efficient scale — natural oligopoly (pipelines, rating agencies).

Score each 0–2; corroborate with 5–10y gross margin and ROIC vs peers.
FATE `buffett_quality` proxies moat via ROE, margins, leverage.

## Formulas
### ROIC vs WACC
$$ROIC - WACC > 0 \Rightarrow value\ creating$$
Moat shows as sustained positive spread.

### Moat duration (concept)
$$Years\ ROIC > WACC\ through\ cycle$$
Longer duration justifies higher terminal multiple.

### Buffett quality score
$$Quality = f(ROE, margins, leverage)$$
FATE composite moat proxy.
*Implemented:* `analytics.value_investing.buffett_quality`

## Screens / checklist
- ROIC > WACC for 5+ years
- Gross margin stable vs competitors
- Pricing power in inflation pass-through
- Share stable or gaining
- At least one identifiable moat source

## Traps & failure modes
- Confusing market share with moat in commodity biz
- Patent cliff without pipeline
- Regulatory moat that politics can revoke
- Peak-cycle ROIC mistaken for structural

## Catalysts
- Moat widening via network growth
- competitor exit

# Management Analysis

*Family: `analysis` · id: `management`*

## Philosophy
Management analysis judges leadership on capital allocation, integrity,
operational execution, and alignment with minority shareholders — because
great businesses with poor stewards destroy value through vanity M&A,
dilution, and accounting games. Philosophy: bet on jockeys when the horse
race is long; one brilliant product cannot offset decades of value-leaking
decisions. Track record matters more than charisma on investor day.

Insider ownership and sensible compensation structures align interests;
empire building and related-party deals do not.

## How it works
Review: proxy DEF 14A (comp, board), insider Form 4 flows, buyback timing
(vs price), M&A ROIC post-deals, earnings call Q&A candor, employee reviews
(Glassdoor), auditor tenure. Red flags: frequent restructuring charges,
auditor switches, promotional accounting metrics.

Score 1–5 on allocation, integrity, operations. Pair with economic_moat —
great management cannot fix no-moat industry indefinitely.

## Screens / checklist
- Insider ownership > 1% for mid-cap+ or meaningful $ for founders
- Buybacks primarily when P < IV estimate
- M&A deals earn ROIC > WACC post-integration
- Board majority independent with relevant expertise
- CEO/CFO low unexplained turnover in C-suite

## Traps & failure modes
- Star CEO cult ignoring board weakness
- Stock comp dilution disguised as talent retention
- Roll-up accounting complexity
- Founder control without minority protection

## Catalysts
- Activist forcing capital return
- management upgrade
- insider cluster buying
